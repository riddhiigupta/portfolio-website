# learnings + recommendations

&nbsp;

<details style="margin:1.4rem 0; border-bottom:1px solid rgba(210,175,110,0.18); padding-bottom:0.5rem;">
<summary style="cursor:pointer; color:rgba(237,232,216,0.95); letter-spacing:0.18em; text-transform:lowercase; font-weight:700; font-size:clamp(0.95rem,1.4vw,1.15rem); padding:0.65rem 0; list-style:none;">▸ learnings</summary>

<p style="font-size:0.85rem; color:rgba(237,232,216,0.7); margin:1rem 0 1.4rem; line-height:1.7;">things i've picked up running clubs, teams, and just trying to figure life out. some are leadership, some are life, some are just how i think.</p>

<h3 style="margin-top:1.6rem; margin-bottom:0.8rem; color:rgba(237,232,216,0.95); font-size:clamp(0.92rem,1.45vw,1.1rem); letter-spacing:0.16em; text-transform:lowercase; font-weight:700; padding-bottom:0.45rem; border-bottom:1px solid rgba(210,175,110,0.22);">on leading + building teams</h3>

- Clubs work very differently from a company — you can hire fast and fire fast at a company, but in a club you only have a limited pool of people to work with. So you need to ensure your attrition is low.
- People work in very different ways. You need to learn to talk to each of them in a way that makes them feel confident and willing to work for you.
- You need to have difficult convos no matter what. There needs to be transparency about what each person is working on so when you're putting in more effort, others are willing to do so too. For that you also need trust in the team that whatever they're doing is for the better of the company at the end of the day — and for that you need to ensure everyone is working towards one overarching goal instead of smaller goals for their own teams.
- You also must be willing to engage in conflict to hold people accountable. When that's not done, trust doesn't build and people do mediocre work. Instead of keeping mediocre people and ruining culture, it's better for them and you to let them go.
- The "one bad apple" thing is very real. If there's one bad person on your team — whether they don't care enough, do subpar work, or talk poorly about you behind your back — and you don't eliminate them, they will take your team down.
- As a founder, if you want to reduce dependency on you and ensure longevity long after you're gone, you need to make systems and write everything down. If there's too much flexibility, people are confused and do whatever they want instead of what's best for the company. With clear systems people can petition to add things that might make it better, but there's something for everyone to follow — clear definition of who does what, what the deliverables are, what the takeaways from the org are, and what happens to someone who doesn't deliver.
- Everyone needs to be at par with the vision of the company. If you have a certain vision but the others don't see or agree with it, there will be a big communication gap which is overall very bad. Everyone needs to be on the same page even if it has to go through serious conflict.
- Try your best to be friends with the people on your team. Knowing about their personal lives and interests helps you find common ground. If you're friends with someone you want to work with them — so this just increases involvement and overall happiness on the team. Do team socials or off-sites often, just hang out outside the formal setting.
- As a founder/president, in the beginning you might do all of it, but as the org gets bigger you just cannot manage the nitty-gritty of every department. You need to build a team you trust. Your job is to grow the company, take it to the next level, and make sure the systems are doing their job.
- If your business is consumer-centric, do your best to not make any major public errors. One of our mistakes: our technical progress was slow in the club, and people (even a lot of members) didn't really mind. But we said we *might* do travel reimbursements — clearly mentioned in all comms that it wasn't guaranteed — and when we couldn't, everyone went crazy. Reddit posts hating us about everything, half of which didn't even make sense. Lesson: if you wrong a lot of people at once it'll be really bad. Minimize that any way you can and keep people happy.
- **Celebrate with your team!!** If everyone's working really hard all the time, you need smaller public rewards — give credit to people for doing a good job — and for every major win, celebrate together. Non-negotiable. Give them a break after if you want, but it comes after the party!
- Take care of your team. Even if you're strict, give them breaks when they ask, be understanding of their situation, and have fun with them instead of bossing them around. They have no reason to stick and work if they don't like you.
- Give people rewards based on what they deserve. If there's a big sponsor or big interview, take the people who really deserve it to the stage and give them the limelight. Don't hog it for yourself. If you're the face of the company you'll have enough of it anyway — give others a chance, they'll feel really special and motivated.

<h3 style="margin-top:2rem; margin-bottom:0.8rem; color:rgba(237,232,216,0.95); font-size:clamp(0.92rem,1.45vw,1.1rem); letter-spacing:0.16em; text-transform:lowercase; font-weight:700; padding-bottom:0.45rem; border-bottom:1px solid rgba(210,175,110,0.22);">life lessons</h3>

- Things will go wrong but honestly there is always a solution. So many things have gone wrong for us with Guinness, Purdue, etc. in StarkHacks but we got through it even if it was stressful.
- Work really hard. You can do *so* much. At one point I was managing sponsorships (getting new sponsors + handling existing ones), full marketing (volunteers, participants), design (flyers, signage), outreach for participants, AND working on hardware for the hackathon. I managed all of that alone for a short time before pulling in my team. Honestly manageable if I didn't have classes.
- **Ask for help!** If something is hard, just ask your colleague. It builds trust — you're not afraid to show your vulnerable side, and it makes them feel smart too.
- **Take feedback!** Genuinely ask for people's opinions on how you're doing, how they're doing. Do these feedback sessions to make sure you're both comfortable with each other and respect each other enough to make changes. Be willing to listen to others' opinions of you and adjust.
- Life is genuinely much easier and fun when you're doing what *you* want to do instead of what people want you to do. My parents wanted me to do a CS major. I hated it but still tried — every hour I spent on it I would dread, and it would take energy away from what I actually wanted to be doing (running my club, doing all the leadership-y stuff). Now that I've switched to robotics — even though everyone was like "it's less prestigious, it's polytech, you won't get a job" — I'm happier and have more time to do the club stuff I love. Bro, you have one life. Make your own mistakes and choices, do what you genuinely want. Think it through, don't make rash decisions, research what happens in each scenario — but choose the one that makes you happy from within. It is so much easier.
- Every relationship in the world is transactional. If you have a mentor giving you so much of their time to make you better at something, they see potential in you they want to nurture and that makes them happy or feel like they're investing in something. If you make a friend, either you make them feel smarter, make them smarter, or you're funny and you make them laugh. You give something (or multiple things) and they give something back.
- Hang out with the right kind of people, always. I surround myself daily with people who work all the time and are highly motivated, because that's what I want my day-to-day to look like. But I also have friends who are fun and funny and can hang out with me on weekends, or are good listeners so they can hear me rant, or have fun drama going on so I get to hear stories and have a good time. Surround yourself with the right people for the right situations. And eliminate the wrong ones in the first go — just stop interacting with them. If you let toxic people in they cause too much bs that you ideally wouldn't have time for.
- Be more straightforward with people. Until very recently I was very shy and afraid to hurt people — "okay I'll manage it just this once, why cause drama" — but bro, saying stuff out always keeps the air clean. Otherwise you dwell on it afterwards and form bad opinions of people based on something they wronged you with once. Open conversations solve major problems.
- **Luck surface area** is a very real thing and it's directly related to how many people know you / about you. The more people know what you're doing and just know you in general (and if they're good quality), the more they'll reach out when an opportunity makes sense — or you'll reach out to them. You'll know about more things, see what fits, and just keep growing.
- You need to get used to saying no — and receiving no from the other end. Whenever I'd reach out to sponsors I'd get nos from the top-most people in the world. Like the CEO of Apple John Ternus — I invited him to our event as a speaker and he said no, but the fact that he replied was insane to me. Same with Jensen Huang. Same with discipline: if my friends wanna hang out too many days a week and I don't have the time, I have to say no even if they get mad.

<h3 style="margin-top:2rem; margin-bottom:0.8rem; color:rgba(237,232,216,0.95); font-size:clamp(0.92rem,1.45vw,1.1rem); letter-spacing:0.16em; text-transform:lowercase; font-weight:700; padding-bottom:0.45rem; border-bottom:1px solid rgba(210,175,110,0.22);">beliefs</h3>

- **Everything in life is an experience.** You could write a piece of code that does the job, but if you have nice visuals and design and a person gets an experience out of it, they remember you because they *felt* something because of you. That's why I made my website like this — it's unique, you'll remember it because it gave you a new experience when you were least expecting it, but it still is fully me and a representation of everything I am and love.
- Your body is the first way people judge your competence before you even speak. Even though we don't actively think we're judging, people do think "wow this guy has a sleek physique, takes care of himself, goes to the gym" — which signals discipline and hard work even before they know anything else about you. Plus people are more likely to favor someone they like, so if you're attractive you'll get shit for free just because.
- **You can turn your life around any time.** I lost my way in high school. I still did stuff but I was failing more than I ever had — bad grades, not too much learning, bad friend circle, completely unmotivated, just doing what my parents asked. Then I came to Purdue and was inspired by what people had done in HS — Aarav who ran a startup and met top entrepreneurs, Aayan who did multiple internships and could build really cool apps. Since then I've been all about building, working hard, and now I'm ahead of a lot of people I know who (in my terms) are wasting their lives like I did back then.
- Be more excited about things in life and try to have as much fun as you can. I work all day, like genuinely as much as I can without any break day. But during those days I take 1–2 hours spread out to talk to my friends and hang out. I also just work *with* them because they also work all day so it's fun. I don't really care about football but whenever I go to a match I enjoy it passionately. Nothing really matters at the end of the day, and whatever you're doing must make you happy. Life is genuinely all about being happy — minimize any time spent towards being unhappy.
- **Follow your passions.** I know it's cliche but trust me, life is way easier when you do that. I used to struggle so much because I was just doing shit I didn't like. Once I started focusing on clubs, giving up internships to build the consumer app I always wanted to do, or saying no to big tech because I wanted to work at a very early-stage startup — things have just always been amazing.
- **Don't be afraid to try out new things and hate it.** I tried CS, didn't like it. Thought I loved EE enough to be an EE major, didn't like it enough. Same with MechE. I loved math but not as something I'd do full-time. I hang out with engineers all the time, so they used to be like "design ew" — but bro, I genuinely love design. I used to be embarrassed about it, then came to accept it as my USP and life is so much better. Same with marketing — I didn't like to be associated as a marketer, but a person who understands tech, has taste, AND can do marketing? That is a superpower, nothing to be embarrassed about. So yeah, you're really cool bro, just accept your passions and don't be embarrassed of it.
- **Don't be afraid to leave books in the middle.** I start a book, don't find it great because either it's not relevant to my situation right now or whatever — doesn't mean it's a bad book, just that I read it and didn't find it useful, and now have a better idea of when I'd need it. *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* — I'd read it when I'm leading a real big company.
